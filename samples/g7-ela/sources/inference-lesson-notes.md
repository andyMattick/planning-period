# Inference week: my lesson notes (Ms. Okafor, 7th grade ELA, made-up sample)

## What I tell them on day 1
An inference is what you figure out from clues the author gives you plus what you already know. The author doesn't
say it outright. My line on the board all week: **Clue + What I know = Inference.** If you can point to the exact
sentence that "says" your answer, it's not an inference, it's a detail. If you can't point to *any* sentence, it's a
guess. An inference lives in between.

Good inferences are *supported*. Two kids can make different inferences from the same story, but both have to point
back to the text. "Which evidence best supports" questions are where my kids lose points: they pick a sentence that's
*true* and *from the story* but doesn't actually prove the inference. I want them to ask: "If this sentence were the
only one I had, would it convince someone?"

## Citing evidence
- Quote it or name the paragraph: "In paragraph 3, the narrator says..."
- Sentence starters on the wall: "The text states...", "For example, in paragraph __...", "This shows that..."
- Evidence alone isn't enough. They have to explain HOW the evidence shows the inference. That's the part most of them
  skip. Claim, evidence, explanation (we call it C-E-E).

## Story idea I want to use (fiction)
A kid (I want a 7th grader, maybe named Marisol or Dev) who is about to perform or compete at something and is
nervous but never says "I'm nervous." Show it through actions: checking things over and over, not eating, snapping at
a younger sibling, then a small moment of kindness at the end that shows she cares about the sibling. There should be
at least two things you can infer about her: she's nervous / anxious, AND she cares about her family even when she's
stressed. Maybe she's a drummer in the school band's winter concert. Keep it short, one scene, a morning in the
kitchen.

Things to make inferable (not stated):
- she is nervous (actions: retying shoes, tapping, not eating)
- the little brother looks up to her (he copies her drumming on the table)
- she feels bad after snapping at him (she goes quiet, then does something nice)
- maybe: the concert matters because someone important is coming (grandmother? first time hearing her play)

## Article idea (nonfiction)
Short informational piece about **city crows** being smarter than people think. Things I've read (keep it general,
nothing that needs a citation beyond "scientists have found"):
- Crows can recognize individual human faces and remember a person who bothered them; studies where researchers
  wore masks found crows scolded the mask years later, and crows that weren't there at first joined in (they learn
  from other crows).
- Crows use tools: bending wire or using sticks to get food (New Caledonian crows especially).
- Some city crows drop nuts on roads so cars crack them.
- Crows gather around a dead crow (some scientists think they're learning about danger).
Questions should ask kids to infer things the article doesn't say outright, like: crows pass information to each
other; the author thinks people should respect crows / underestimate them; why crows might gather at a dead crow.
Keep it at a 7th grade level, about the length of the story.

## What I want on the quiz
- Mostly inference and "which evidence best supports" (paired questions are good: Part A inference, Part B evidence,
  but I don't want Part B to give away Part A).
- A couple of "what does this detail suggest" and one about why the author included something.
- End with a short constructed response: What can you infer about [the character]? Use two pieces of evidence.
- Version A and B: same passages (I only have time to teach two), different questions on the same skills.

## Rubric notes
4 points. I score C-E-E: a clear inference (not just a detail retold), two pieces of evidence that really support it,
and explanation that connects them. Kids self-check with it before turning in, so write it in "I" statements
they understand. 4 = all three strong, 1 = an inference with no real support or just retelling.

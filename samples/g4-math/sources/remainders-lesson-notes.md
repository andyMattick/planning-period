# Week 9 notes: Multi-step problems and "what do I do with the leftover?"

*Mr. Lindqvist, 4th grade math, Thursday co-op group. My own notes for the week, not a handout.*

## Where we are

The kids can multiply up to 4 digits by 1 digit and 2 digits by 2 digits, and they can divide up to 4 digits by
1 digit with a remainder (area model or partial quotients; a few like the standard way). This week is about putting
those together in stories, and about the part they always rush: **the remainder means something.**

## Big idea 1: Read the story twice, then make a plan

I keep saying the same three things:

1. **What do I know?** Circle the numbers and say what each one counts ("6 eggs in a carton", not just "6").
2. **What am I finding?** Underline the question. Say it in your own words.
3. **Plan the steps.** Many stories need two steps. Find the first answer, then use it.
   Write a letter for the unknown if it helps: "Let *n* be the number of cartons."

Example we did together: *The farm stand sold 4 baskets of 28 apples on Saturday and 75 apples on Sunday. How many
apples in all?*  Step 1: 4 × 28 = 112. Step 2: 112 + 75 = 187 apples. One equation: 4 × 28 + 75 = *a*.

Then: *Is 187 reasonable?* Round: 4 × 30 = 120, plus about 80 is 200. Close to 187, so yes.
I want an estimate check on every Level 3 problem.

## Big idea 2: The remainder — four things it can mean

Same division, different stories. 46 ÷ 5 = 9 R1.

| The story asks… | What we do | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 46 kids, 5 per canoe. How many canoes so everyone goes? | **Round up**: the leftover kid still needs a canoe | 10 canoes |
| 46 eggs, 5 per bag. How many full bags can we sell? | **Drop it**: the leftover can't make a full bag | 9 bags |
| 46 stickers shared by 5 kids. How many are left over? | **The remainder is the answer** | 1 sticker |
| 46 feet of rope cut into 5 equal pieces? | Not this week (that's fractions — 5th grade) | — |

How I tell them to decide: **picture the last group.** Does the leftover still need a place (round up)? Is the
question about full or complete groups (drop it)? Is the question about what's left (the remainder)?

Common mistakes I saw last week:
- Writing "9 R1 canoes." A canoe count can't be "R1". Answer in a sentence with the unit.
- Always rounding up, even for "full bags."
- Giving the quotient when the story asks "how many are left?"
- Doing only the first step of a two-step story and stopping.
- Adding when the story needs multiplying ("each", "every", "per" usually mean equal groups).

## Big idea 3: Two steps with a remainder in the middle or at the end

Harder kind: *The co-op has 3 boxes of 48 granola bars. We pack 7 bars in each snack bag. How many full bags?*
Step 1: 3 × 48 = 144 bars. Step 2: 144 ÷ 7 = 20 R4 → 20 full bags (drop the 4).
Check: 20 × 7 = 140, and 140 + 4 = 144. ✔

Another: *63 people are going on the field trip. Each van holds 8 people. Each van costs $45 for the day. What's the
cost?* Step 1: 63 ÷ 8 = 7 R7 → 8 vans (round up — those 7 people still need a seat).
Step 2: 8 × $45 = $360. A kid who drops the remainder gets $315, and leaves 7 people at home.

## Comparing and "times as many" (quick review)

"3 times as many" means multiply: The pumpkin patch has 4 times as many small pumpkins as large ones. 36 large →
4 × 36 = 144 small. "How many in all?" is then a second step: 144 + 36 = 180.

## Numbers I like for 4th grade stories

- Eggs in cartons of 12 (or 6), muffins in boxes of 4 or 6, jars in cases of 8 or 12.
- Prices in whole dollars ($3 a jar, $12 a crate), so the money stays whole numbers.
- Seats: cars hold 4 kids, vans hold 8, a minibus holds 15. Tables seat 6.
- Keep dividends under 1,000 for division, products under 10,000. One-digit divisors only.

## What I want from any practice they do

- Easy ones first, then the skill on its own, then two-step ones.
- In **each** level, at least one problem where the remainder changes the answer (one round up, one drop it, one
  "how many left" across the sheet).
- Answers as numbers they write in the blank, plus a few multiple choice for the quiz so they're ready for the
  state test.
- Stories that sound like our life: the farm stand, the co-op, the library, field trips, the garden.

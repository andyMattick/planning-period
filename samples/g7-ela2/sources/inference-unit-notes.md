# Inference and evidence unit: my notes (Mr. Haverford, 7th grade ELA, made-up sample)

## The big idea I teach
Authors leave things unsaid on purpose. An inference = what the text shows + what I already know about people or the
world. On the board all unit: **"The text shows ___, so I can figure out ___."**
- A *detail* is something the text says straight out. If you can underline the answer word for word, it's not an
  inference.
- A *guess* has no text behind it. If you can't point to a sentence, it's a guess.
- A strong inference is the one that fits **all** of the clues, not just one. Readers who stop at the first clue
  usually get the trap answer. I tell them: "Read to the end before you decide. Authors like to change your mind."

## Best evidence
"Which quotation best supports..." is where my students lose the most points. They pick a quotation that is true and
on topic but only *mentions* the subject; it doesn't *prove* the claim. Test: "If I showed someone only this
quotation, would they agree with my answer?" Cite with the paragraph number in brackets: [4].

## RACE for constructed responses
Restate the question, Answer it, Cite evidence (two pieces, with paragraph numbers), Explain how each piece proves the
answer. The E is what they skip.

## For this quiz
### Story (fiction), please write one
Something small and everyday, one scene, a 7th grader. I like stories where the character's feelings change and the
reader has to notice it from actions and what people *don't* say. Ideas I've used before and liked: a kid who is
asked to look after something that belongs to someone else; a misunderstanding between friends or with a neighbor
that the reader figures out before the character does. No sports or music performance stories (we did those).
Things I want them to infer: how a character feels without being told; why a character does something; what a
character learned or how they changed; what the ending means.

### Article (nonfiction), please write one
A short science or nature article. I'd like **sea otters and kelp forests**: otters eat sea urchins, urchins eat
kelp, so where otters disappeared (hunted for fur in the 1700s-1800s) urchins took over and kelp forests shrank; where
otters came back, kelp grew back. Keep facts general and true (it gets checked). Things to infer: why the author
includes a detail; what the author wants readers to think; cause and effect the article doesn't spell out; what a
word means from context.

## What I don't want
- Questions you can answer from the question alone.
- Part B choices that are made-up quotes. Every Part B choice must be a real sentence from the passage.
- Trick wording, "all of the above", "none of the above".
